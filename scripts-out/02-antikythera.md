## COLD OPEN

>> Fragment A, museum lighting, extreme close on corroded bronze

This is the oldest computer in the world, and for most of the last century nobody could read it.

>> Pull back slowly to reveal the whole fragment

It was found by Greek sponge divers in 1901. [doi:10.1038/s41598-021-84310-w] [doi:10.22323/1.170.0018] In the early years, no-one knew what it was. [doi:10.22323/1.170.0018]

That is not a figure of speech. For decades this object sat in a museum in Athens as a corroded lump of bronze that some people thought was an astrolabe, some thought was a navigational instrument, and some thought had fallen into the wreck from a much later ship, because the alternative was impossible.

>> The 82 fragments laid out on black

Today it is split into 82 fragments, and only a third of the original survives — including 30 corroded bronze gearwheels. [doi:10.1038/s41598-021-84310-w]

Thirty gears. Cut by hand. Two thousand years ago.

>> Gear teeth, macro, rotating

Nothing else like it survives from antiquity. Nothing close. The next machine of comparable mechanical sophistication appears in Europe more than a millennium later.

And here is the part that should unsettle you: we did not decode it by finding more of it. We decoded it by learning to see inside what we already had.

## PROMISE

>> X-ray CT slice, translucent bronze, gears emerging from the corrosion

In 2005, a small British X-ray company took an eight-tonne cabinet to Greece. [doi:10.22323/1.170.0022]

What came out of it changed the history of technology.

>> Text characters emerging from inside the metal

Microfocus X-ray computed tomography let researchers see beneath the corrosion, count gear teeth, and read inscriptions which had lain unread for 2,000 years at the bottom of the Mediterranean. [doi:10.22323/1.170.0022]

Thousands of tiny text characters, buried inside the fragments. [doi:10.1038/s41598-021-84310-w]

>> Slow assembly animation of the mechanism

This is the story of how a machine explained itself, in its own words, from inside a block of corrosion — and what it turned out to be doing.

## ACT I — WHAT IT DOES

>> Reconstruction, front dial, hand turning a knob

Start with the object as a machine, before the mystery.

Using a handle — probably similar to a doorknob — the user could insert an input date on a dial on its front side. [doi:10.22323/1.170.0007]

That is the entire user interface. You set a date. You turn a crank.

>> Gear train turning, cutaway

The calculations were made using a set of gears, at least thirty of them, and the results were displayed on several scientific scales. [doi:10.22323/1.170.0007]

>> Sun, Moon, planets, orbital diagram

It was used to calculate the diurnal and annual motion of the Sun, the Moon, and most probably the planets among the stars. [doi:10.22323/1.170.0007]

It put into practice the astronomical knowledge of the ancient Greeks about the motion of these objects with astonishing accuracy — including eccentric gearing for the anomalous orbit of the Moon. [doi:10.22323/1.170.0007]

>> The Moon speeding up and slowing down, animated

Stop on that. The Moon does not move at a constant speed across the sky. It accelerates and decelerates over its orbit. That irregularity is called the lunar anomaly, and it is a genuinely hard thing to model.

This machine models it. Mechanically. With bronze gears, one of which is deliberately mounted off-centre so that its rotation speeds up and slows down in step with the real Moon.

>> Eclipse, total solar, archive

It could also predict eclipses of the Sun and the Moon, from the Saros period, which was found on one of its back scales. [doi:10.22323/1.170.0007]

And it calculated the dates of the Olympic Games, and of the other major crown games of ancient Greece. [doi:10.22323/1.170.0007]

>> Olympic stadium ruins, present day

That last function is the one that tells you what this object was for. A machine that predicts eclipses is an instrument of astronomy. A machine that also tells you when the games are is an instrument of *civilisation* — it is keeping the calendar of an entire culture, sacred and civic at once.

## PIVOT — SEEING INSIDE

>> The fragments again, damaged, unreadable

Now the problem.

The major surviving fragments are labelled A to G, and the minor fragments 1 to 75. They are partial, damaged, corroded and covered in accretions. [doi:10.1038/s41598-021-84310-w]

Fragment A alone contains 27 of the surviving 30 gears, with a single gear in each of Fragments B, C and D. [doi:10.1038/s41598-021-84310-w]

>> Fragment A rotating, opaque

So almost the entire gearbox is inside one lump of metal that you cannot open, cannot see into, and must not damage.

The fragments are, in the words of the team that solved them, a 3D puzzle of great complexity. [doi:10.1038/s41598-021-84310-w]

>> X-Tek cabinet being lowered by crane, reconstruction

Which brings us to 2005, and the eight-tonne cabinet.

X-Tek Systems, at the time a small UK-based X-ray company, developed a high-resolution, high-voltage X-ray source and took it to Greece. [doi:10.22323/1.170.0022] Their CT was not medical CT. Medical scanners are built to image soft tissue at low doses. This needed to punch through solid corroded bronze and resolve detail at the millimetre level.

>> CT reconstruction building up slice by slice

In 2005, microfocus X-ray computed tomography and Polynomial Texture Mapping of the Mechanism's 82 fragments added substantial data. [doi:10.1038/s41598-021-84310-w]

>> Gear teeth being counted on screen, numbers appearing

They could now count teeth on gears they had never seen. They could read letters carved on plates buried inside a solid block.

The fragments are rich in evidence at the millimetre level — fine details of mechanical components and thousands of tiny text characters, buried inside and unread for more than 2,000 years. [doi:10.1038/s41598-021-84310-w]

>> The back of the machine, solved, animated

This led to a solution to the back of the machine, with the discovery of eclipse prediction and the mechanisation of the lunar anomaly. [doi:10.1038/s41598-021-84310-w]

The back was solved. And then the work stopped, for fifteen years, because of the front.

## ACT II — THE FRONT

>> Front of the mechanism, mostly missing, dark

The front remained deeply controversial, due to loss of physical evidence. [doi:10.1038/s41598-021-84310-w]

This is the honest state of the problem. The back survived well enough to be read. The front did not. Most of the front gearing is simply gone — dissolved into the seabed over two millennia.

>> Inscription plate, letters visible in CT

But the machine had left instructions.

Reconstructing the Cosmos at the front begins with analysing some remarkable inscriptions: the Front Cover Inscription and the Back Cover Inscription. [doi:10.1038/s41598-021-84310-w]

>> Text overlay, Greek characters, translated

The Back Cover Inscription describes the front display as a Planetarium — a Cosmos arranged in rings, with planets marked by "little spheres" and the Sun as a "little golden sphere" with a "ray" and a "pointer". [doi:10.1038/s41598-021-84310-w]

>> Rings of the Cosmos, animated, gold

Read that again, because it is extraordinary. The people who built this machine wrote down what it looked like when it worked. On the cover. Like a manual.

Two thousand years later, with the mechanism itself destroyed, that description is how we know what the missing parts were doing.

>> Planetary cycles, numbers on screen

The Front Cover Inscription lists the synodic cycles of the planets — the cycles relative to the Sun. It is a systematic list, itemising the synodic events and the intervals in days between them, with the planets written in the same geocentric order as the Back Cover Inscription. [doi:10.1038/s41598-021-84310-w]

>> A reconstruction failing to fit, red

And here is where it got difficult. Inscriptions specifying complex planetary periods forced new thinking on the mechanisation of this Cosmos — and no previous reconstruction had come close to matching the data. [doi:10.1038/s41598-021-84310-w]

Every model built before had been wrong. Not slightly wrong. It did not match the numbers the machine itself had written on its own cover.

## RECAP

>> Fast montage of the previous beats

So: an object recovered in 1901 that nobody could identify. Eighty-two fragments, a third of the original, thirty surviving gears, twenty-seven of them locked inside a single lump. A tomography rig in 2005 that read the writing inside the metal. A back that gave up eclipse prediction and the lunar anomaly. A front that did not.

And an inscription, carved by the builders, describing a machine that no reconstruction could produce.

## ACT III — THE SOLUTION

>> The 2021 model, assembling, precise

In 2021, a team led by Tony Freeth published a new model.

Their discoveries led to a model satisfying and explaining the evidence. Solving this complex 3D puzzle, they wrote, reveals a creation of genius. [doi:10.1038/s41598-021-84310-w]

>> Gear ratios, prime factorisations on screen

The method is worth understanding, because it is not archaeology in the shovel sense. It is closer to cryptanalysis.

The surviving inscriptions give certain planetary periods. Others are missing. The team showed that the missing periods for Mercury and Mars are uniquely determined by their process. [doi:10.1038/s41598-021-84310-w]

>> Single solution highlighted among alternatives

Uniquely determined. Given the constraints — accuracy, factorisability into gear teeth, and economy of design — there is exactly one answer the builders could have used.

For Jupiter there were two additional options sharing a prime factor. The team showed one was not possible and the other very unlikely. [doi:10.1038/s41598-021-84310-w]

>> Venus mechanism, epicycle, animated

And then there is the physical evidence that confirms it.

No other surviving gear in the Mechanism has a disk attached. [doi:10.1038/s41598-021-84310-w] In an inferior planet mechanism, a pin-and-slotted follower requires a pin attached to the epicyclic gear but beyond its edge — and this attached disk is exactly the right size to carry that pin at the correct distance from the centre to model the maximum elongation of Venus. [doi:10.1038/s41598-021-84310-w]

>> The disk, macro, then the Venus orbit

A single anomalous disk. On one gear. The right size, to a millimetre, to do one specific job.

>> The bearing on Spoke B

There is one more, and it is my favourite.

There is a prominent bearing on one spoke of the main drive wheel whose purpose nobody had ever explained. The 2021 model shows a solution: the bearing enables a four-gear epicyclic system that calculates the lunar nodes — the points where the Moon's orbit crosses the Sun's path, which is what determines whether an eclipse happens. [doi:10.1038/s41598-021-84310-w]

And the proposed tooth counts mean the bearing sits in exactly the right place on that spoke. [doi:10.1038/s41598-021-84310-w] No other use had previously been found for it. [doi:10.1038/s41598-021-84310-w]

>> The model completing, all systems turning

This is what a correct reconstruction looks like. Not a plausible story — a model that predicts, in advance, where an unexplained piece of hardware must be, and then finds it there.

## RESOLUTION

>> The complete mechanism turning, slow, reverent

With its astonishing lunar anomaly mechanism, the Antikythera Mechanism emerges as a landmark in the history of technology and one of the true wonders of the ancient world. [doi:10.22323/1.170.0018]

The latest model includes a mechanical representation of the Cosmos that exactly matches the inscription on the back cover of the instrument. [doi:10.22323/1.170.0018] The researchers believe we are now close to the complete machine. [doi:10.22323/1.170.0018]

>> Portraits: Rehm, Price, Wright, Freeth

It took a hundred years, and it took a chain of people who each solved part of it: Albert Rehm, Derek de Solla Price, Michael Wright, and most recently the members of the Antikythera Mechanism Research Project. [doi:10.22323/1.170.0018]

Not one of them could have finished it alone. Rehm could not have had CT. Price could not have had the inscriptions. The 2021 team could not have started from scratch.

>> The single object, alone, on black

And here is the thing that makes this object different from almost every other discovery in science.

There is only one of them. [doi:10.22323/1.170.0047]

>> Contrast: many astronomical objects vs one artefact

That is a genuine epistemic problem, and one astronomer has put it precisely. In astronomy, the study of a *collection* of objects greatly aids our understanding — you find one strange thing, then you find forty more, and the forty teach you what the one meant. [doi:10.22323/1.170.0047]

With the Antikythera Mechanism, there is no second example. There never has been. Every conclusion has to be wrung out of one damaged artefact, and there is no way to check it against another.

## CLOSE

>> Seabed, dark water, slow drift

Two thousand years ago, someone in the Greek world could build this.

They could model the irregular motion of the Moon in bronze. They could predict eclipses. They could track five planets. They could write the cycles on the cover so that the owner would know what they were looking at.

>> The gap: a timeline, empty for a thousand years

And then the capability disappears from the record for over a millennium.

We do not know who made it. We do not know how many were made. We do not know what else that workshop could do, because nothing else from it survived — this object exists at all only because a ship sank, and the sea kept it.

>> The fragments, museum, present day

Which raises the question the object actually asks, and it is not "how did they do it".

It is: what else did they do that we do not know about, because no ship happened to sink at the right moment?

>> Slow fade on Fragment A

The Antikythera Mechanism is not evidence of a lost civilisation of superscience. It is evidence of something more ordinary and more disturbing — that our picture of ancient technology is built almost entirely on what happened to survive, and that survival is close to random.

One shipwreck moved the known history of gearing back by a thousand years.

There is no reason to believe it was the only machine of its kind. There is only a reason to believe it was the only one that sank in the right place.
